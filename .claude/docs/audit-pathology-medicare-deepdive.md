# Deep dive: audit trail architecture, automated pathology detection, and Medicare number handling

*Prepared 13 August 2026 — first pass at technical direction ahead of the low-level solution architecture.*

## 1. Audit trail for specialist actions — the instinct is right, the mechanism should change

The requirement itself is correct and important: an action like "specialist responds with advice — full appointment avoided" needs to be immutable, attributable to a specific specialist, and legally defensible if ever disputed. That's not optional for a platform sitting in a clinical pathway. Where I'd push back is the mechanism — a Bitcoin-style distributed ledger with multi-node consensus solves a different problem than the one you have, and building the whole platform that way would trade away real things (speed, privacy, cost, operational simplicity) for a property you don't actually need.

**What Bitcoin-style consensus is actually for.** It exists to let mutually distrusting, often anonymous parties agree on a shared record with no central authority at all — that's the specific, hard problem multi-node consensus solves, and it's expensive to solve: slow writes, high operational cost, and every participating node holding a copy of the ledger. None of that describes your situation. You are a real, accountable, regulated legal entity. Every specialist on the platform is a known, AHPRA-verified, licensed individual. There's no need to pay consensus's cost to get trust between parties who already have real-world legal accountability — and worse, a shared ledger replicated across nodes is close to the opposite of what a health platform should want for patient data, which the earlier identity/security work already flagged should be minimised and access-controlled, not multiplied across copies.

**What actually delivers what you're describing — non-repudiation, tamper-evidence, "the specialist stamps it with his own key" — without the distributed-consensus cost:**

- **A digitally signed, append-only, hash-chained audit log.** Every legally or clinically significant event (referral created, viewed, advice given, consent changed, delegate access granted) becomes an immutable record. Each record is digitally signed with the acting party's own private key, so the specialist genuinely cannot later deny having made that entry — this is exactly the "PKI stamp" idea, just using real cryptographic signing rather than a blockchain transaction. And each record includes a cryptographic hash of the one before it, so any retroactive edit or deletion breaks the chain and is immediately detectable — that's the "immutable, can't be quietly altered" property people associate with blockchain, achieved without needing a network of nodes to agree on anything. This pattern has a name and prior art outside crypto — Certificate Transparency logs, Amazon's QLDB (Quantum Ledger Database), and tools like Google's Trillian and immudb are all built exactly this way: centrally operated, cryptographically tamper-evident.
- **Use Australia's own existing healthcare PKI rather than inventing one.** NASH (the National Authentication Service for Health) already exists specifically to "authenticate and securely access digital health services, digitally sign documents and other transactions, and encrypt health information for secure exchange," issued to healthcare provider organisations. ([NASH PKI — Murray PHN](https://murrayphn.org.au/focus-areas/digital-health/nash-pki-certificates/)) That's real, government-run, already-trusted infrastructure that specifically supports the "specialist signs the transaction" property you want — worth building the signing layer on top of NASH-issued credentials rather than issuing your own certificates from scratch, and it's another concrete item for the ADHA/vendor conversation (how a platform like this actually gets its own NASH-backed identity and how specialists' existing NASH credentials could be used for in-platform signing).
- **Optional extra layer, if you want belt-and-braces:** periodically publish a hash of the current audit chain to a neutral, public timestamping service. This can genuinely use a public blockchain purely as a notarisation beacon (this is a known, narrow technique — sometimes called timestamp anchoring) to prove "this chain state existed at this time, and not even we could have backdated it" — without running the whole platform as a blockchain. Worth considering later, not needed for v1.

**Net recommendation:** don't architect the whole platform as a distributed ledger. Build a proper signed, hash-chained audit log as a dedicated layer that every module writes to — referrals, bookings, consent, and the "advice given" event in question all pass through it — while the operational data (the actual referral records, the booking calendar, patient profiles) lives in a normal, well-indexed database designed for fast reads and writes. The audit layer is the accountability record sitting behind the operational system, not the operational system itself.

## 2. How the automatic pathology-result detection actually works, mechanically

Checked the concrete plumbing rather than leaving this at "it detects automatically," since the two paths behave quite differently in practice.

**My Health Record access is pull-based, not push-based.** Based on the FHIR Gateway's documented interaction model, an application requests an access token and then queries for a patient's documents — there's no confirmed webhook or push notification when a new document (like a pathology result) is uploaded. ([My Health Record FHIR Gateway — Digital Health Implementer Hub](https://implementer.digitalhealth.gov.au/resources/services/my-health-record/my-health-record-fhir-gateway)) Practically, that means "automatic" via this path means a scheduled poll — the platform checking periodically (say, daily, starting a few days before the expected test date) for a new pathology document matching the Follow-up Plan item — not an instant, real-time trigger. Set expectations accordingly: same-day-ish detection, not seconds-after-the-result-lands detection.

**The faster path — being a registered recipient on the result message itself — is more work but closer to real-time.** When a GP or specialist orders a pathology test electronically through the existing e-ordering rails, the result message typically supports more than one addressee. If the platform is registered as an additional recipient on that specific order, the result arrives the moment the lab processes it, the same way it arrives at the ordering clinician. But this requires the platform to actually be a legitimate, addressable participant in this ecosystem, not just a website — concretely: registering the platform itself for a **Healthcare Provider Identifier for Organisations (HPI-O)** through the Healthcare Identifiers Service (the same system that assigns HPI-I to individual practitioners and IHI to patients — see below), and a NASH certificate to authenticate and encrypt that traffic. ([Healthcare Identifiers Service — Digital Health Implementer Hub](https://implementer.digitalhealth.gov.au/resources/services/healthcare-identifiers-service-hi)) That's a foundational registration step worth sequencing early — it's a prerequisite for several things already discussed (secure messaging participation, My Health Record conformance, NASH-backed signing above), not a one-off task specific to pathology.

**Recommended sequencing:** ship the My Health Record polling path first — it needs less cooperation from third parties (just the patient's consent and the platform's own conformance work, both already on the roadmap) — and treat direct secure-messaging registration as the faster v2 once the platform has its own HPI-O and NASH identity anyway. Self-report stays the permanent fallback for anything done at a private or interstate lab outside these rails, as already recommended.

## 3. Is it okay to keep the patient's Medicare number on file?

Yes, storing it is legal and normal for a platform genuinely operating in this space — a referral platform has a real health-service purpose for holding it (identity matching, supporting claiming, appearing on referral documents), which is a completely different context from the well-known restriction on *non-health* businesses demanding a Medicare card as general-purpose photo ID (that controversy and the resulting restrictions were specifically about retailers, video stores, and similar collecting it outside a health context — not about health providers using it for its actual purpose). That said, three things matter for doing it properly:

- **It's sensitive information under the Privacy Act, so APP 11 security-safeguard obligations apply in full** — encryption at rest and in transit, access controls, and it needs to be in scope for the Notifiable Data Breaches scheme if it's ever compromised. Nothing here is different from any other identifying health information already covered in the first identity/security document, but it's worth naming explicitly rather than assuming it's covered by implication.
- **Purpose limitation matters.** Use it only for what it was collected for — identity matching and claiming/referral purposes — and don't let it drift into general-purpose use (a marketing identifier, a login credential, a de-duplication key for unrelated features). That discipline is what keeps this squarely inside "legitimate health-service use," which is the context where holding it is uncontroversial.
- **Better architecture: don't make it the platform's actual identity key.** Australia already has a purpose-built alternative — the **Individual Healthcare Identifier (IHI)**, issued under the Healthcare Identifiers Service specifically so systems like My Health Record can match patients accurately "as opposed to an individual's Medicare number," precisely because reusing a billing number as an identity key is the wrong tool even though it's tempting. ([OAIC — Healthcare identifiers](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-legislation/related-legislation/healthcare-identifiers)) Recommended pattern: use the IHI as the platform's internal patient-matching key, and store/surface the Medicare number only where it's actually needed (claiming, referral documents) rather than as the thing every other record is keyed against. That reduces what's exposed if any single record or view is ever compromised, and it's the architecturally correct separation between "who is this person" and "what's their billing number" — two different things that shouldn't be conflated even though it's convenient to.
- **Whichever way it's stored, every access to it should itself generate an entry in the audit log from section 1** — viewed, not just changed — since "who looked at this patient's Medicare number and when" is exactly the kind of question an audit trail needs to answer under a Notifiable Data Breaches investigation or a patient complaint.

## Follow-up: where does the IHI actually come from?

Worth being precise about the question here, because it changes the answer: nobody needs to "get" a new IHI for the vast majority of patients — it already exists. An IHI is **automatically assigned to almost everyone already** — anyone enrolled in Medicare or holding a DVA card was already given one when the system was set up, and it's assigned automatically to new enrolments now. Someone without Medicare/DVA cover can apply for one directly through myGov (with a passport plus valid visa, or a driver's licence) or via a paper form if they lack standard ID. ([How to get an Individual Healthcare Identifier — Services Australia](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/how-to-get-individual-healthcare-identifier?context=22591)) So the real question for the platform isn't "how does a patient get one," it's "how does the platform resolve the IHI that already exists for a given patient" — and there are two paths, worth sequencing the same way as the pathology-result detection above.

**Path A — piggyback on the GP's existing practice software (do this first).** This isn't a hypothetical integration to build — it already exists and general practices already use it routinely. Best Practice (Bp Premier), for instance, has a built-in "Bulk Health Identifier Look Up" function: staff select a date and provider, the software matches patients against the HI Service using first name, last name, date of birth, and Medicare card details, and returns each patient's IHI with a colour-coded confidence result — PHNs recommend practices run this daily. ([IHI Look-up Best Practice — Brisbane South PHN](https://bsphn.org.au/documents/bulk-hi-look-up-bp-final.pdf)) Since the platform already plans to integrate with GP practice software for referrals and MyMedicare status (the Tier B/C integration discussed earlier), the IHI can ride along on that same connection rather than needing its own separate project — the same "reuse the integration you're already building" pattern that applies to MyMedicare enrolment status too.

**Path B — the platform resolves IHIs directly (the deeper, independent version).** Once the platform has its own HPI-O and NASH certificate — already flagged as a foundational registration step for the pathology-result and audit-signing work above — it can query the HI Service's own web services directly, matching on the same name/DOB/Medicare details, rather than depending on any specific GP software vendor's cooperation. This is the more resilient long-term path, but it's genuinely the same prerequisite work already on the list, not a new registration project.

**Path C — let the patient/carer self-service it, as a first-class feature, not just a fallback.** myGov has its own "Link Individual Healthcare Identifiers service" option, so a patient can look up and confirm their own IHI directly. ([Link Individual Healthcare Identifiers service — myGov](https://my.gov.au/en/about/help/mygov-website/link-services-to-your-account/link-individual-healthcare-identifiers-service)) Worth building this into the patient/carer profile properly rather than treating it as an edge case: a "Verify my details" section where the patient or their consented carer can view what the platform has on file (Medicare number, IHI if resolved, DOB) and correct it themselves — self-entered, then verified against the HI Service the same way the provider-side lookup works (name/DOB/Medicare match), not just accepted on trust. This fits the patient-centred design principle already established for this platform: the patient owns and can correct their own record, rather than being a passive subject of whatever a GP's system happened to resolve. It's also genuinely useful operationally — a patient who hasn't yet got a GP practice fully connected to the platform, or whose demographic-matched lookup came back ambiguous, still has a path to a resolved, verified IHI rather than being stuck.

## 4. Preventing duplicate accounts and fraud — and why this is where the audit log actually earns its keep

This is the right thing to be worried about, and it's a real, well-documented problem, not a hypothetical one. Worth grounding the design in two facts rather than intuition: this exact discipline has a name in health IT — **Master Patient Index (MPI) / Enterprise Master Patient Index (EMPI) deduplication** — precisely because every health system with more than one entry point into patient records runs into "did this person already have a record under a different clinic/spelling/mobile number" as a routine operational problem, not an edge case. ([Enterprise master patient index — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enterprise_master_patient_index)) And on the fraud side specifically: identity crime costs Australia an estimated $3 billion a year and affects around 1.7 million Australians annually according to the Australian Federal Police, and — the statistic that matters most for this platform's specific design — **almost half of medical identity theft is committed by a relative or someone already known to the victim**, not a stranger. ([Medical identity theft, a worrying diagnosis — Cited](https://cited.com.au/medical-identity-theft-a-worrying-diagnosis/)) That second statistic is exactly the risk the carer/patient onboarding design from the very first conversation in this project was built to address — it's not a theoretical edge case, it's the dominant real-world pattern, which is a good reason to treat that original design as core rather than optional polish.

**The "patient moves GPs" scenario, concretely.** When a different GP triggers a new-account request for someone who already has an active ReferralPlatform account, the platform needs to catch this at the point of request, before a duplicate account is created — this is precisely what EMPI deduplication logic is for. The right key to deduplicate on is the **IHI**, not name/DOB/mobile matching alone (which produces false matches and false non-matches at real-world scale — common names, shared family mobiles, changed numbers) and not the Medicare number alone (a Medicare card covers a family unit, not a single unique individual, so it's a weaker deduplication key than it looks). Concretely: resolve the IHI first (via Path A/B/C above), then check whether an active platform account already exists against that IHI before creating anything new. If one exists, route the new referral into the existing account and notify the existing account holder that a new GP has referred them — never silently create a second identity for the same person.

**Where this needs judgement, not just a lookup:** a match should be surfaced for review, not always auto-resolved silently — if the new referring GP is one the platform has never seen for this patient before, that's worth a lightweight verification step (e.g., confirming with the patient/carer that yes, they've started seeing this GP) rather than blind trust, precisely because the "already known to the victim" fraud pattern above means the request itself could be the fraud, not just the account-matching logic getting it wrong.

**On the GP side of this, not just the patient side:** the platform should only accept new-account and referral-creation requests from properly authenticated healthcare provider systems — meaning requests carry a genuine HPI-O/NASH-backed identity, not just an email or a web form a bad actor could fill in claiming to be a GP practice. That closes the other half of the fraud surface: it's not enough to stop a fraudulent *patient-side* account grab if the *GP-side* trigger itself isn't authenticated.

**This is exactly what the audit log from section 1 is for, concretely applied.** Every new-account trigger, every deduplication decision (matched to existing account / created new / flagged for review), and every carer/delegate grant should itself be a signed, timestamped, hash-chained entry — signed with the triggering GP's NASH-backed credential on the provider side, and logged against the patient/carer's own action on the consumer side. That means if a fraudulent account-creation attempt or an improper carer claim ever happens, there's a non-repudiable record of exactly which credential initiated it, when, and what the platform's deduplication logic decided and why — which is precisely the evidence trail needed to investigate a dispute or support a legal/police referral if genuine fraud occurred, and it's the concrete answer to "ReferralPlatform should be fully trusted": trust here comes from every consequential action being attributable and undeniable, not from a promise.

# Referral Platform — Business Case, Competitive Landscape, and Integration Strategy

*Prepared 13 August 2026*

## Bottom line up front

The referral *workflow* between GPs and specialists in Australia is already substantially digitised and has been for well over a decade — it runs on a handful of entrenched secure-messaging networks (HealthLink, Medical-Objects, ReferralNet/Telstra Health, Argus, HealthShare) that are pre-integrated into every major GP practice system. That is the real competitive moat, and it's a plumbing/distribution moat, not a features moat. Your genuine white space is not "electronic referrals" — it's the **patient-owned account, carer/consent management, and single-point traceability layer** that none of the incumbents currently offer, because they are pure business-to-business message routers with no patient-facing identity at all. The business model needs to be built around that differentiator, not around competing head-on for GP desktop real estate with a transaction fee — the market has already shown it resists new fees on referral sending, even small ones.

## 1. Who the competition actually is (Australia)

**Secure messaging / e-referral routing networks** — the incumbent layer every GP system already talks to:
- **HealthLink** — long-established, embedded as a partner product inside Best Practice and MedicalDirector; recently moved from "referrals are free" to a small annual subscription ($12/year Essential, $480/year Professional) and got significant GP pushback over it (see section 4). ([HealthLink AU](https://www.healthlink.com.au/products/healthlink-messaging-system/), [GPs still unhappy with HealthLink pricing — Medical Republic](https://www.medicalrepublic.com.au/gps-still-unhappy-with-healthlink-pricing/125635))
- **Medical-Objects** — the other dominant secure messaging vendor, deeply integrated into practice software, markets itself partly on cost savings versus paper/fax. ([Medical-Objects](https://www.medicalobjects.com/))
- **ReferralNet (Telstra Health)** and **Argus (Telstra Health)** — secure messaging networks used heavily by hospitals and specialists, particularly for discharge summaries and referrals into the public system. ([ReferralNet](https://www.referralnet.com.au/about-us))
- **HealthShare Digital** and **SR – Specialists & Referrals (Cervin)** — specialist-directory-plus-referral products that sit closer to what you're describing (searchable specialist directories with referral routing), already integrated with practice software like Zedmed. ([HealthShare Digital](https://www.healthsharedigital.com/solutions/referrals/), [Zedmed integrates with HealthShare and SR](https://www.zedmed.com.au/news/zedmed-integrates-with-healthshare-and-sr-specialists-referrals/))

**What none of these do:** give the *patient* an account, a login, or a consent-management surface. They move HL7/FHIR referral documents between provider systems. The patient is a data subject, not a user. That gap is exactly where your platform's items 1 and 6 (patient consent page, patient account) live — it's real differentiation, not a me-too feature.

**Maturity assessment:** this incumbent layer is mature (10–20+ years), profitable, government-endorsed (listed under the Australian Digital Health Agency's secure messaging program), and already wired into practice software vendors' partner marketplaces. ([Secure messaging — Digital Health Agency](https://www.digitalhealth.gov.au/healthcare-providers/initiatives-and-programs/secure-messaging)) You will not out-integrate them from a standing start. You can, however, sit on top of or alongside them.

## 2. Are they already connected to myGov / My Health Record?

Not in the direct sense you mean. My Health Record has its own separate "Conformant Clinical Software Products" register — practice software and some secure messaging tools can be certified to upload/view documents in My Health Record, and Medical-Objects specifically offers a My Health Record viewer inside its own product. ([Conformant clinical software products — Digital Health Agency](https://www.digitalhealth.gov.au/healthcare-providers/initiatives-and-programs/my-health-record/conformant-clinical-software-products), [My Health Record in Explorer — Medical-Objects](https://www.medicalobjects.com/myhealthrecord-in-explorer/)) But this is document upload/retrieval, not the "single connection point for traceability" you're describing — there is no existing platform giving MyGov a unified, patient-consented view of the referral chain itself. That specific piece — traceable, patient-consented referral provenance surfaced through a myGov-style channel — genuinely does not appear to exist yet as a product. It's the strongest part of your pitch, and it's also the part that would need a formal conversation with the Australian Digital Health Agency / Services Australia, since myGov integration isn't something you can build unilaterally — it requires their partnership or accreditation pathway (the TDIF process referenced in my last note is the relevant mechanism for identity; document exchange with My Health Record has its own separate conformance process).

## 3. Connecting to the GP's desktop: standalone vs. embedded, and what it costs

There are three realistic paths, in increasing order of integration depth and cost:

**A. Standalone web app, copy/paste or PDF upload.** Cheapest and fastest to build — the GP finishes their referral in their own practice software as normal, then manually pastes the text or uploads the letter into your platform. Zero integration cost, zero vendor negotiation. The cost is adoption friction: this is an extra manual step for every referral, competing against a status quo that's already a single click inside the GP's existing software via HealthLink/Medical-Objects. Every e-referral effort that has succeeded at scale (HealthLink, e-RS in the UK) succeeded specifically by removing this friction, not by adding a second step. Use this only as a bootstrap MVP, not the target end state.

**B. Downstream of the existing secure messaging rails.** Register your platform as a receiving endpoint on HealthLink/Medical-Objects/ReferralNet (or ask a GP software vendor to route a copy) so referrals already being sent through the existing pipes also land in your platform, where you add the patient account, consent, and AI layer. This is materially cheaper than building native integrations into four separate practice software products, because you're integrating with three or four secure-messaging networks instead of a dozen-plus practice systems, and those networks are built to route to registered endpoints. It also sidesteps a head-on turf fight with the incumbents — you become a value-added destination, not a replacement.

**C. Native "Send to ReferralPlatform" button inside the GP software itself.** The deepest integration and best UX — this is the "linked into the GP software" option you asked about. It requires going through each vendor's partner/marketplace program (Best Practice, MedicalDirector, Zedmed, and Genie Solutions all have documented partner ecosystems and existing HealthLink/Medical-Objects listings as the model to match). ([Zedmed integrations](https://www.zedmed.com.au/integrations/), [HealthLink on Best Practice's partner network](https://bestpracticesoftware.com/partner-network/our-partners/healthlink/), [HealthLink on MedicalDirector marketplace](https://www.medicaldirector.com/marketplace/healthlink)) None of these vendors publish integration fees; in practice this is a bilateral negotiation involving conformance testing against HL7v2/FHIR interfaces, a certification cycle, and often a revenue-share or listing arrangement rather than a flat fee. Budget for months of vendor relationship-building and technical certification per vendor, not a simple API key sign-up. Practical sequencing: build path B first to get real referral volume and prove the consent/AI value-add, then use that traction to negotiate path C with the one or two dominant vendors (Best Practice and MedicalDirector between them cover the large majority of Australian general practice).

## 4. Does the pricing model stack up?

Your proposed model — free to patient/carer, GP pays 10c per referral sent, specialist pays nothing to receive, specialist pays 5c if they initiate a referral — is clean and easy to explain, but two things from the research above should give you pause before committing to it:

**GPs are demonstrably fee-resistant here, even at trivial amounts.** HealthLink's move from genuinely free e-referrals to a $12/year "Essential" subscription triggered enough backlash to be covered repeatedly in the medical trade press ("HealthLink fees debacle," RACGP coverage of the new subscription). ([More than money at stake in HealthLink fees debacle — Medical Republic](https://www.medicalrepublic.com.au/more-than-money-at-stake-in-healthlink-fees-debacle/113535), [HealthLink reveals new subscription fees — newsGP](https://www1.racgp.org.au/newsgp/professional/healthlink-reveals-new-subscription-fees)) The historical market expectation, per that same reporting, is that referral sending is free to the GP because the *receiving* side (hospitals, specialists) has traditionally funded the messaging infrastructure. A model that charges the referrer, even at 10c, inverts that expectation and — however financially trivial per-transaction — is likely to draw the same kind of organised pushback (RACGP, AMA) that a much larger subscription fee did. Framing and who's billed matters more than the dollar amount here.

**The revenue is small relative to what it costs to build and run this.** Using the numbers below, national penetration at 10c/5c per referral is a low-single-digit-millions-per-year revenue line — plausibly enough to cover a lean SaaS operation, but thin for something that also needs to fund security/privacy compliance (Privacy Act, TDIF-grade identity proofing, possibly formal accreditation with My Health Record/ADHA), 24/7 reliability expectations (this sits in a clinical pathway), and AI feature development.

**Alternative or blended model worth considering:**
- Charge the **specialist practice** a modest subscription (not per-referral) for the structured data, reduced admin, and AI triage — this is where HealthLink itself is now finding it can charge (its Professional tier), and specialists have a much clearer, quantifiable time-saving benefit to point to than GPs do.
- Position the **consent/traceability layer** as a public-good component and pursue **PHN or Australian Digital Health Agency funding** for it — PHNs already fund secure messaging rollout in general practice today, so there's a funding precedent for exactly this kind of infrastructure. ([Secure messaging for primary healthcare providers — SWSPHN](https://swsphn.com.au/what-we-do/secure-messaging-for-primary-healthcare-providers/))
- Keep the GP-side fee if you want the discipline of a paid product, but make it a small flat annual/practice-level fee rather than a per-referral micro-transaction — per-transaction fees on clinical actions tend to read (fairly or not) as "a tax on referring patients," which is a bad headline regardless of the actual dollar amount.
- A specialist-initiated-referral fee (your item 4) makes sense left as-is — it's a smaller, less politically sensitive volume, and specialist-to-specialist referral is closer to a discretionary administrative action than a GP's core clinical duty.

## 5. Referral volumes — Australia and internationally

**Australia:** AIHW reports **35.6 million Medicare-subsidised specialist attendances in 2024–25**, up from 28.8 million in 2014–15 (~24% growth over the decade), across 8.9 million patients. ([AIHW — Referred medical specialist attendances](https://www.aihw.gov.au/reports/medical-specialists/referred-medical-specialist-attendances)) Note this counts *attendances*, not unique referral letters — in Australia a single specialist referral is typically valid for 12 months and can cover multiple attendances, so the number of actual referral documents generated per year is meaningfully lower than 35.6 million; a reasonable planning estimate is somewhere in the ballpark of 15–20 million referral events/year, though I couldn't find an authoritative figure for that specific metric and you should treat it as an estimate, not a sourced number.

**Is this a global pattern?** Yes — GP/primary-care gatekeeping to specialist care, and the resulting referral-management problem, exists everywhere with a gatekeeper-style primary care system (UK, most of Europe, Canada, Australia, NZ). It's less pronounced in systems without mandatory gatekeeping (much of the US, where patients can often self-refer to specialists, though referral-driven insurance networks recreate a similar dynamic).

**Countries with mature e-referral platforms already:**
- **UK (NHS e-Referral Service, "e-RS")** — the most mature national example. Under the NHS Standard Contract, GPs in England are contractually required to use e-RS for essentially all referrals to consultant-led outpatient services — i.e., near-universal mandated adoption, not organic uptake. NHS England publishes open weekly data on referral volumes, bookings, and specialty/provider breakdowns. ([NHS e-Referral Service — NHS England](https://www.england.nhs.uk/digitaltechnology/connecteddigitalsystems/nhs-e-referral-service/), [e-RS open data dashboard](https://digital.nhs.uk/dashboards/ers-open-data)) This is the model to study if you want to see what "fully digitised, universally mandated, government-run" looks like at scale — the caveat is it took a government mandate to get there, not a commercial fee model.
- **Canada** — actively building toward this (Ontario's eReferral/eConsult programs, e.g. Think Research's offering), but it's fragmented by province and still has real gaps in specialist-side adoption; academic literature repeatedly cites "facilitators and barriers to implementing electronic referral systems" as an open problem, not a solved one. ([Transforming the specialist referral and consultation process in Canada — CMAJ](https://www.cmaj.ca/content/191/15/E408), [Think Research eReferrals](https://www.thinkresearch.com/ca/products/ereferrals/))
- **Denmark and the Netherlands** are frequently cited in the health-informatics literature as advanced adopters of structured electronic referral/e-communication between primary and secondary care, generally as part of broader national health IT infrastructure rather than a standalone commercial referral product.
- **US** — highly fragmented; referral management is mostly solved inside large integrated health systems' own EHRs (Epic, Cerner) rather than by a single national platform, because there's no single national primary-care gatekeeper system to standardise around.

**Implication for you:** every country that has actually achieved near-universal e-referral adoption did it through a government mandate or a dominant, government-endorsed national scheme (UK e-RS, Australia's existing secure messaging program), not through a standalone commercial startup winning market share GP-by-GP. That's a strong argument for treating ADHA/PHN partnership and public-good framing as central to your go-to-market, not an afterthought.

## 6. Other domains: justice, family/community services referrals

This pattern — a caseworker refers a person to another organisation, and someone wants a closed loop showing the referral was received and actioned — is exactly the "coordinated care" / "closed-loop referral" category that already exists and is reasonably mature in the social-services sector, both in Australia and internationally:

- **Infoxchange (Australia)** already runs this kind of platform for government human services. Their "oneplace" product is a public directory of roughly 46,000 services that caseworkers use to refer clients, and they built a full client/case management and referral system for the **Queensland Department of Communities, Child Safety and Disability Services**, following recommendations from the Queensland Child Protection Commission of Inquiry — it now links 16 family and child connect hubs, 30+ child and family support services, and 90+ youth support services statewide. That's a genuinely mature, operational, statewide deployment, not a pilot. ([Infoxchange — Keeping children and families safe](https://www.infoxchange.org/au/impact-stories/keeping-children-and-families-safe), [Infoxchange — Service Coordination & Referral](https://www.infoxchange.org/au/products-and-services/service-coordination)) Infoxchange also runs **Ask Izzy**, the consumer-facing self-search directory for people experiencing homelessness or hardship. I couldn't confirm from search whether St Vincent de Paul specifically uses an Infoxchange product versus its own internal case management system — worth a direct check with Vinnies if this domain is a real target.
- **In the US**, **findhelp.org** (formerly Aunt Bertha) and **Unite Us** are the two dominant "closed-loop social care referral" platforms, both integrated into major EHRs (Epic, Cerner) so a clinician or caseworker can refer a patient to a food bank, housing service, or family support org and see whether the referral was actually actioned — this is the health-and-social-care convergence version of exactly what you're proposing, and it's already a well-funded, multi-state, VC-backed category with real traction (state Medicaid contracts, health-system partnerships). ([findhelp.org](https://www.findhelp.org/), [Unite Us — providers](https://uniteus.com/industries/providers/))

**Is it working effectively?** Reasonably well at the "directory + tracked referral" level — the Queensland deployment and the findhelp/Unite Us traction both suggest the core pattern (searchable service directory + trackable referral + shared case record) is proven technology, not an open research problem. Where it's weaker, based on the literature, is exactly the same identity/consent question from your first message: family and justice referrals frequently involve **mandatory, non-consensual information sharing** (a child protection referral, or a justice-system referral, doesn't wait for the subject's consent the way a health referral does). That means the elegant patient-consent model from your health platform doesn't transfer directly — these domains instead run on statutory information-sharing schemes (e.g., Victoria's Child Information Sharing Scheme) with legally defined thresholds for when consent can be overridden in the interest of safety. If you expand into this space, budget for that as a distinct legal/governance track, not a config toggle on the same consent page.

**My take:** the underlying "referral plumbing" (create referral → notify receiving org → track status → close the loop) is genuinely reusable across health, justice, and community services, and building it domain-agnostically from day one is a reasonable long-term architecture choice. But go-to-market should stay sequential, not parallel — prove the model in health first, where you already have a concrete GP/patient/specialist workflow and a possible ADHA relationship, before taking on the different legal regime that justice and child-protection referrals require.

## 7. Follow-up: PHN and ADHA, explained

**PHN = Primary Health Network.** Thirty-one regional, government-funded, non-clinical organisations that between them cover the whole of Australia. They don't deliver care themselves — they commission and coordinate primary care services regionally (GP support, mental health programs, chronic disease management, closing service gaps) and are funded by the federal Department of Health, Disability and Ageing. ([What PHNs do — Dept of Health](https://www.health.gov.au/our-work/phn/what-PHNs-do?language=en)) The funding precedent I flagged is concrete, not hypothetical: individual PHNs already run named "secure messaging" support programs that help local general practices get set up on HealthLink/Medical-Objects — e.g. Country to Coast QLD's "Digital health program – Secure Messaging," South Western Sydney PHN's secure messaging support page, and Darling Downs and West Moreton PHN's equivalent. ([Country to Coast QLD](https://c2coast.org.au/digital-health-program-securemessaging/), [SWSPHN](https://swsphn.com.au/what-we-do/secure-messaging-for-primary-healthcare-providers/)) That's the literal budget line to ask your local PHN to extend.

**ADHA = Australian Digital Health Agency.** The Commonwealth agency that runs national digital health strategy and infrastructure — it operates My Health Record, sets national interoperability/conformance standards, and has run its own initiative specifically to get the competing secure messaging vendors (HealthLink, Medical-Objects, ReferralNet, Argus) interoperable with each other rather than staying siloed — described in the press as an effort to "axe the fax." ([ADHA partners to standardise secure messaging platforms](https://www.govtechreview.com.au/content/gov-digital/news/adha-partners-to-standardise-secure-messaging-platforms-789031926), [ADHA rolls out secure messaging initiative to axe the fax](https://www.healthcareitnews.com/news/anz/adha-rolls-out-secure-messaging-initiative-axe-fax)) That existing initiative is worth building against directly rather than negotiating bilaterally with each vendor.

## 8. Follow-up: what HealthLink actually charges

The current structure (confirmed from HealthLink's own subscription update page) is deliberately **flat-rate per practice, not per-message or per-GP** — worth noting given your original per-referral pricing idea:

- **Essential — $12/year per practice.** Includes SmartForms, referrals, and diagnostics at no extra cost, plus "improved security and contractual protections."
- **Professional — $40/month per practice** (~$480/year). Adds new features rolling out progressively; currently free as a promotion until early 2026.
- Practices on an existing legacy arrangement can stay on it unchanged — nothing is forced.
- There is explicitly **no volume-based or per-doctor charge** — a solo GP and a twenty-doctor practice pay the same flat fee per tier.

([HealthLink — GP Subscription Update](https://www.healthlink.com.au/gp-subscription-update/)) This matters for your model: HealthLink, the incumbent that already tried a usage-linked approach in the past and got burned by GP backlash, has deliberately landed on flat per-practice pricing rather than per-transaction. That's a second, independent signal (beyond the general "referrals should be free" expectation) pointing toward a flat annual fee rather than a 10c-per-referral micro-transaction if you want a paid GP tier at all.

## 9. Follow-up: who to actually talk to for myGov / My Health Record integration

Two separate asks, with two separate (and very differently sized) pathways:

**A. Accepting government Digital ID (myID) as a login option — the lighter path.** You don't need to become an accredited identity provider yourself to let patients or carers log in with a government-verified identity. You can integrate as a "relying party" that accepts credentials from an already-accredited provider (myID, or another TDIF-accredited private provider like the ones referenced in my last research). This is a standard integration, not a government negotiation — start at the Digital ID System site's relying-party documentation. ([Trusted Digital Identity Framework — Digital Identity](https://www.digitalidentity.gov.au/tdif))

**B. Becoming accredited yourself, or getting a formal myGov/traceability partnership — the heavy path.** This is the one that genuinely requires a government relationship, not a self-service sign-up. Two concrete entry points:
- **My Health Record document conformance** — ADHA runs a formal testing and conformance program for software that reads/writes My Health Record documents, with a published vendor declaration form and a "Testing and conformance" resource hub on their Implementer Hub. This is the pathway for the "specialist doesn't need to scan paper, GP has traceability" part of your platform. ([My Health Record Conformance Vendor Declaration Form](https://developer.digitalhealth.gov.au/resources/my-health-record-conformance-vendor-declaration-form-v1-0), [Testing and conformance — Digital Health Implementer Hub](https://implementer.digitalhealth.gov.au/resources/resource-topics/testing-and-conformance))
- **Formal TDIF accreditation** (if you ever want to *be* a recognised identity provider rather than just accept myID logins) is a multi-stage, independently-assessed process documented in the "TDIF 03 Accreditation Process" release. It is not something to pursue at MVP stage — it's a mid-to-late roadmap milestone once you have real traction, and option A above is very likely sufficient for what you actually need. ([TDIF 03 Accreditation Process](https://www.digitalidsystem.gov.au/sites/default/files/2023-07/tdif_03_accreditation_process_-_release_4.8_-_finance_2.pdf))

**Practical first move:** ADHA publishes a "Partnership Pulse" newsletter and runs an active industry/vendor engagement channel through its Digital Health Developer Portal — that's the door to knock on for an introductory conversation, not a cold enquiry to a general contact form. Go in asking two specific questions: (1) what's the conformance pathway for a new clinical software product that writes referral documents to My Health Record, and (2) does ADHA have an existing pilot, sandbox, or working group for patient-consent-linked referral traceability that a new entrant could plug into — a production myGov-level connection on day one is very unlikely; a sandbox or pilot invitation is the realistic first outcome. ([Partnership Pulse — Edition 5](https://www.digitalhealth.gov.au/newsroom/partnership-pulse-newsletter/partnership-pulse-edition-5))

## 10. Follow-up: how to actually pursue PHN/ADHA funding, step by step

1. **Identify your local PHN** (of the 31 covering Australia) and find their digital health/secure-messaging program page — several already publish one, as shown above. That team already has a budget line for exactly this category of work.
2. **Frame the ask as an extension of what they already fund**, not a new category: "this adds patient consent and traceability on top of the secure messaging infrastructure your program already supports," tied to PHN's actual KPIs — care coordination, reducing avoidable hospital presentations, closing service gaps. Use the two documents already produced in this project as the concept brief.
3. **In parallel, request an introductory conversation with ADHA's industry/partnership team** (contact via the Digital Health Developer Portal / Partnership Pulse channel referenced above) with the two specific questions from section 9.
4. **Check for open grant rounds** rather than assuming one exists right now — the Medical Research Future Fund (MRFF) has funded digital health trials before, and PHNs periodically run their own regional innovation/digital-uplift grant rounds; these open and close on their own schedules, so this needs a live check against health.gov.au and your local PHN's site rather than relying on this document.
5. **Set realistic expectations on timeline.** This is a government-relations process measured in months, and the realistic sequence is: small regional pilot with a handful of GP clinics and specialists (PHN-funded) → evidence from that pilot → ADHA conversation about conformance/sandbox access → only then a conversation about anything resembling a national myGov-level connection.

## 12. Follow-up: what to actually learn from the UK, Denmark/Netherlands, and Canada

**UK — NHS e-Referral Service.** Three transferable lessons, and one that isn't:
- *Not transferable:* the near-100% adoption came from a contractual mandate under the NHS Standard Contract, not organic product-market fit. You won't replicate that without a government or PHN mandate behind you.
- *Transferable — "Advice and Guidance":* GPs can ask a specialist a clinical question through the same referral system before committing to a full referral; the specialist either answers directly or converts it into a booked referral. This is a genuinely reusable feature idea for your platform, and it's a natural fit for the AI-enablement goal in your project brief — AI-assisted triage suggestions with a human specialist sign-off, sitting in front of a full referral rather than after one.
- *Transferable — referral and booking as one workflow:* e-RS grew out of the older "Choose and Book" system, so referral creation and appointment booking were designed together rather than as separate steps. Worth considering whether your platform should let the patient pick a specialist/date within the same flow, rather than treating booking as the specialist's separate problem.
([NHS England — NHS e-Referral Service](https://www.england.nhs.uk/digitaltechnology/connecteddigitalsystems/nhs-e-referral-service/))

**Denmark — MedCom.** The most relevant lesson here is governance, not features. MedCom has run Denmark's national health-data-exchange standards and infrastructure for roughly three decades, and its defining characteristic is that it's a neutral, consensus-governed body — not a single company's proprietary platform — bringing together government, regions, and GP organisations to agree standards rather than one vendor imposing them. ([MedCom — Aalborg University research portal](https://vbn.aau.dk/en/publications/facilitating-hie-in-denmark-the-story-of-medcom-a-danish-health-i/)) That maps directly onto the "public-good infrastructure" positioning already recommended above: a traceability layer co-governed with government, GP bodies, and specialist colleges is likely to earn trust (and funding) faster than the same thing pitched as one company's commercial product. I did not find enough in this round of research to give you a confident, specific Netherlands comparison beyond the general pattern that some European systems (the Netherlands' referral-management market is more commercially vendor-led) show a single commercial platform *can* reach near-universal adoption if it achieves deep GP-software integration — a path closer to what HealthLink has done in Australia than to MedCom's consortium model. Worth a dedicated follow-up if the Netherlands specifically matters to you.

**Canada — Ontario's eConsult program.** This is the strongest evidence base of the three, and directly reusable. In its first two years (mid-2018 to mid-2020): 60,474 consults submitted, monthly volume up 181%, specialists responded in a median of one day (95.9% within a week), and — the number that matters most for a funder's business case — **51% of consults avoided an originally planned specialist referral entirely.** Nearly 4,500 primary care clinicians and 1,400+ specialists took part; specialists billed a median 15 minutes and about $50 per case; only 2% of requesting clinicians found the response unhelpful. ([The Provincial Spread and Scale of the Ontario eConsult Service — Annals of Family Medicine](https://www.annfammed.org/content/20/3/262)) Two things to take from this: first, an "ask a specialist before you refer" feature (the same idea as NHS e-RS's Advice and Guidance, but with harder outcome data behind it) is worth building early, because it gives you a concrete, quantified pitch to PHNs/ADHA — "this reduces unnecessary specialist referrals and wait times," which is a far stronger funding argument than a transaction fee. Second, Ontario funds this by paying specialists a small fee-for-service amount per eConsult response out of the health system's own budget — i.e., the payer is the system, not the referring GP — which is a real-world precedent for the "fund it as infrastructure, not user-pays" model recommended above.

## 13. Follow-up: connecting to the GP's desktop via a watched folder / drag-and-drop

Yes, this is workable, and it isn't a new idea in this specific industry — it's worth knowing the precedent before building it. Practice software with no real API has historically been integrated by secure messaging vendors (Medical-Objects, HealthLink) using a **virtual printer driver**: the GP "prints" the referral from their existing software to what looks like a normal printer, but is actually a driver that intercepts the print job, converts it, and routes it automatically through secure messaging. That's a mature, well-understood pattern in exactly this market, and it's the version of your idea I'd actually build toward rather than inventing something from scratch.

Three tiers, roughly in order of how quickly you could ship them:

1. **Browser drag-and-drop upload.** Simplest possible MVP — no local software install, GP finishes their letter and drags the file into your web app. Doesn't remove the "leave your GP software, go find the file" step, so treat it as week-one bootstrap only, matching the "standalone" tier from section 3 above.
2. **Watched folder with a lightweight desktop agent.** A small installed agent watches a designated folder; the GP saves the referral there (or the practice software is configured to save there by default) and the agent auto-uploads it via API. The critical design requirement: the agent must delete or encrypt the local copy immediately after a confirmed successful upload — a folder full of unencrypted patient letters sitting on a clinic PC is a real data-security liability, not a minor detail.
3. **Virtual printer driver.** The incumbent-proven approach — GP "prints" directly to a "ReferralPlatform" printer instead of navigating to a folder at all. This matches muscle memory GPs already have from HealthLink/Medical-Objects, and is the strongest UX of the three.

Whichever you build, treat it as part of your compliance boundary, not a convenience add-on bolted on afterward: the installer needs to be code-signed and distributed through a channel GP-practice IT admins and antivirus software will actually trust (an unsigned random .exe will get blocked or flagged); it needs a clean, verifiable uninstall; and it needs the same security review as the rest of the platform, since once it's installed on a clinic PC it's handling PHI locally, however briefly. Sequence it after your Tier B integration from section 3 is validated — this is a UX-polish investment worth making once you have real GPs to polish for, not before.

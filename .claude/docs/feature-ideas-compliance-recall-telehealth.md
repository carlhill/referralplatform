# Referral Platform — compliance checks, care-continuity recall, on-demand telehealth referrals, and follow-up-as-eConsult

*Prepared 13 August 2026*

## 1. Compliance-checking referrals against state and federal legislation

Strong instinct, and it extends the pluggable policy-layer architecture already recommended for Phase 2 (justice/social services) into a genuine value-add for complex health referrals too — but it needs a careful framing to avoid creating a new liability problem while solving an old one.

**What the legal landscape actually looks like, so the compliance engine has the right shape:** there isn't a single federal ruleset that state rules sit "underneath" in a simple hierarchy. The Commonwealth Privacy Act 1988 (Australian Privacy Principles) sets a national baseline for how private-sector health providers collect, use, and disclose health information, and applies uniformly regardless of state. On top of that, each state has its own health records legislation (Victoria's Health Records Act 2001, NSW's Health Records and Information Privacy Act, and equivalents elsewhere) and, for child safety/family violence specifically, its own statutory information-sharing scheme — the ones catalogued in `state-by-state-information-sharing-schemes.md` in this project. So a compliance check for a flagged referral (child-related, DV-related, or otherwise high-risk) genuinely needs two layers: a baseline Privacy Act/APP check that applies everywhere, and a state-specific overlay selected by where the patient and receiving organisation actually are.

**The liability-safe way to build this: decision support, not a certification.** Don't design this as software that outputs "this referral is compliant" — that's close to giving legal advice, and if it's wrong in a case involving a child's safety, that's a serious exposure for you, not just the clinician. The value-adding, defensible version is a structured checklist/prompt engine that surfaces the right questions at referral time for flagged categories: is the receiving organisation a recognised "prescribed body" / "information sharing entity" under the relevant state scheme? Does this specific disclosure fall within that scheme's permitted purpose? Is any of the information being shared in an "excluded information" category that the scheme doesn't cover? The clinician or caseworker still makes the actual judgment call — the platform's job is to make sure they're asking the right question and to log that they considered it, which is genuinely useful for audit and defensibility without your platform taking on the legal determination itself.

**Business case:** this is a real, distinct value proposition from the patient-consent layer — it reduces professional liability exposure for clinicians who currently have to personally remember which of eight-plus different state schemes applies to a given case. That's a premium-tier, specialist/agency-facing feature ("Compliance Assist") that could support its own subscription pricing, separate from whatever the base GP/specialist model ends up being — and it's a cleaner sell than a transaction fee, closer to how the market already prices HealthLink's Professional tier. It absolutely needs sign-off from actual legal counsel on the underlying ruleset per jurisdiction before it ships, not just this research.

## 2. Specialist-set follow-up plan → proactive GP/patient reminder

This is probably the strongest of the ideas raised so far, and it's grounded in a real, specific gap rather than a generic "reminders would be nice" feature.

**The gap that actually exists today:** GP practice software already has recall/reminder functionality (Best Practice, MedicalDirector and others all support internal patient recalls — for pap smears, immunisations, GP-initiated follow-ups). What doesn't exist today is a structured, closed-loop way for a *specialist's* follow-up instruction to reach that system across the organisational boundary. Right now, "come back in a year, get bloods in March" typically lives only in a letter the GP may or may not systematically diarise, and in the patient's own memory — which is exactly the failure mode described in idea 4 below. That's the real product to build, not "generic reminders."

**Design:** give the specialist a structured "Follow-up Plan" field when they close out a referral in the platform — not free text, a first-class object: next review date, required pre-visit investigations (a specific test panel, imaging, with a recommended lead time before the appointment), and whether a fresh referral will be needed or an existing/indefinite one still applies (see the referral-validity note in section 4 below — this matters more than it looks). The platform then automates three things a set number of weeks out: notify the GP practice, ideally pushed into their own existing recall/EHR system via the same HL7/FHIR integration already planned for referrals, so it shows up in a workflow they already use rather than a new app to check; notify the patient directly by SMS/app; and pre-populate the pathology/imaging request so the GP only has to review and sign rather than start from scratch. Digital pathology requesting ("eRequests"/"eOrdering") is already a mature, PHN-promoted feature in Australian general practice, not something to build from scratch — worth integrating with that existing rail directly. ([Electronic Pathology Requests — Western Vic PHN](https://westvicphn.com.au/health-professionals/health-topics/digital-health/electronic-pathology-requests/), [eOrders/eRequests — DDWMPHN](https://www.ddwmphn.com.au/eorders-or-erequests))

**Consent note:** this needs to sit inside the same consent framework as everything else — a patient/carer needs to have opted in to proactive contact and to the specialist's follow-up note being used this way. It should be a configurable category on the consent page already designed, not switched on by default.

**Framing for funders:** "reduces lost-to-follow-up" and "closes care gaps" is close to verbatim PHN language from their own stated KPIs — this feature is a genuinely strong item to lead with in the PHN funding conversation recommended earlier, arguably stronger than the base referral-routing pitch, because it's a continuity-of-care story rather than a plumbing story.

## 3. Patient-initiated urgent referral request with telehealth availability window

Also strong, and it reuses infrastructure you're already planning (the queued-referral flow from the original project brief) rather than requiring something new.

**Flow:** patient submits a request in-app — "referral to Dr [Specialist], I'm free Tuesday/Thursday 2–4pm for a telehealth call" — which lands in the GP practice's existing inbox/task workflow (again, better integrated into their existing system than as a separate portal), the practice triages and books the slot, the GP conducts the telehealth consult and issues the referral directly in the platform.

**A real Medicare constraint to design around, not just a footnote:** MBS telehealth rebates for GP consultations require an "existing and continuous relationship" — in practice, the patient needs to have had a face-to-face visit with a GP at that same practice within the preceding 12 months, with specific exemptions (COVID isolation, homelessness, urgent after-hours care, Aboriginal Medical Services, under-12-months-old, some blood-borne-virus/sexual-health services). ([MBS telehealth items and the 12-month rule — NWMPHN](https://nwmphn.org.au/news/mbs-telehealth-items-and-the-12-month-rule-faqs-for-general-practice/)) This means the feature works cleanly for existing patients of a practice, but the platform should actively check and surface this — flagging up front when a patient isn't eligible for a rebated telehealth consult and would need an in-person visit first, rather than letting them submit a request that a Medicare audit later flags as non-compliant. That's a small but important piece of UX, not just a disclaimer.

**Clinical governance point:** a patient marking their own request "urgent" isn't a clinical triage decision, and shouldn't be treated as one. The GP or practice needs to be the one who actually decides urgency and whether a referral is warranted — the platform's job is structured intake and prioritisation support (potentially AI-assisted flagging of concerning symptoms for the triaging nurse/receptionist), not an automatic guarantee of same-week booking or an auto-issued referral. Keeping the GP firmly in the loop also keeps this on the right side of both clinical safety and Medicare's requirement that a rebated consultation actually occurred.

**Positioning:** this is effectively a "digital front door" competing with phone-based booking, which is a genuine, well-known pain point in Australian general practice (time on hold, phone tag). Likely to land well with both patients and practice reception staff.

## 4. Your own example — and the direct link to the Ontario eConsult data

This is worth taking seriously as more than an anecdote — it's precisely the failure mode section 2 is designed to fix, and it also extends the earlier Ontario eConsult research in a way worth calling out explicitly.

**On the immediate pain point:** the "Follow-up Plan" object in section 2, plus digital pathology e-ordering instead of a paper form, directly solves what you described — no paper form to keep safe, no need to personally remember the date, because the platform tracks it and prompts both you and your GP.

**On whether that $295 visit needed to happen in person at all:** this is where it connects back to the Ontario eConsult numbers from the last round of research. Your specialist's outcome was, in substance, "bloods are fine, continue as you are, next full review in 13 months" — a low-complexity confirmation, not a case requiring hands-on examination or a complex new decision. That's exactly the category of interaction the Ontario data shows can often be handled without a face-to-face visit at all: once the platform has a structured Follow-up Plan and the GP has the blood results attached to the record before the "visit" is even due, the specialist could, for a stable/low-risk case where the results are in range, close the loop with a short asynchronous note — "results in range, continue current management, next full review September 2027" — instead of booking a full in-person consultation. That's not a new idea layered on top of what's already been discussed; it's the same eConsult mechanism from section 4 of the last research doc, applied to *follow-up* monitoring rather than *new* referrals, and it's arguably an even better fit here, since routine "stable, nothing's changed" monitoring reviews are precisely the lower-acuity, lower-complexity case type that asynchronous review handles well.

**One more thing worth flagging while we're here — referral validity:** Australian specialist referrals last 12 months by default, starting from your *first* visit with the specialist, not the date the GP wrote it. But GPs can also issue an **indefinite referral** for a particular ongoing condition and specialist/specialty — used for genuinely chronic, stable conditions — which removes the annual re-referral requirement entirely, not just the reminder problem. ([How long does a specialist referral last — NextClinic](https://nextclinic.com.au/blog/how-long-does-a-specialist-referral-last-in-australia)) Worth having the platform actively prompt "this looks like a stable, ongoing condition — would an indefinite referral be more appropriate than annual reissue?" at the point a GP or specialist is about to generate another routine annual referral. Some of the friction you experienced (a whole extra referral cycle to manage) may be solvable just by making that existing option more visible and easier to use, independent of anything else being built.

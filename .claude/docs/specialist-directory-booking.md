# Referral Platform — specialist directory data and closing the booking gap

*Prepared 13 August 2026*

## Where to get a list of specialists, locations, and consulting days

Three tiers, worth combining rather than picking one:

**1. National Health Services Directory (NHSD) — the primary source to build on.** Run by Healthdirect Australia and funded by Australian governments, this is described as "a comprehensive, national directory of health services and practitioners," and it has a public Developer and Integration Hub with APIs specifically for accessing service-provider and practitioner information. ([National Health Services Directory — Healthdirect](https://about.healthdirect.gov.au/what-we-do/portfolio/nhsd), [Healthcare Services API Guide](https://developers.nhsd.healthdirect.org.au/docs/consumer-api/index.html)) I could not confirm the exact coverage of consulting-day/schedule-level detail or the access/licensing terms from what's public — that needs a direct registration/enquiry through their developer hub rather than assuming. This is the right first call to make, since it's the actual government-endorsed national directory rather than a commercial one you'd have to license from a competitor.

**2. AHPRA's public register — for verifying a specialist is real and currently registered, not for locations.** The Australian Health Practitioner Regulation Agency maintains the national public register of every registered health practitioner, searchable by name and profession. ([AHPRA — Register of practitioners](https://www.ahpra.gov.au/Registration/Registers-of-Practitioners)) Useful as a verification/trust layer (confirm a specialist someone is referring to is actually currently registered and in good standing) but it's a regulatory register, not a practice-location or booking directory.

**3. Commercial directories — useful for booking-relevant detail (locations, days, availability), but each is a competitor's dataset.** HealthShare Digital and SR/Cervin (both flagged in the earlier competitive analysis) are specialist directories with referral routing built in; HotDoc and HealthEngine are patient-facing booking platforms with their own specialist listings and live availability. Worth checking licensing terms on any of these before building on top of them, since some may see a referral platform as a direct competitor rather than a data customer. Specialist college "find a fellow" directories (each college — surgeons, physicians, etc. — publishes its own) are a slower but authoritative supplementary source for verifying subspecialty and fellowship status.

**Recommended approach:** start with NHSD as the base layer (government data, no competitive conflict), supplement with AHPRA for registration verification, and treat commercial directories as a "nice to have" data-quality improvement once you have real traction and can negotiate from a position of some leverage rather than needing them for a bare MVP.

## The booking gap — you've identified a real, well-known pain point

"The patient has to call to make the appointment" after a referral is a widely reported friction point in the Australian system — and there's already a live example of two different ways to close it, worth learning from both:

**HotDoc's model (already live) is patient-initiated online booking with the referral attached, not GP-to-specialist automation.** When a specialist practice uses HotDoc, patients can book directly online rather than phoning, and the referral document uploads as part of that same booking flow. ([HotDoc — Specialist bookings and discovery](https://practices.hotdoc.com.au/specialist-practices/)) That's a genuinely useful, already-mature building block — for any specialist practice already on HotDoc (a meaningful share of the market), your platform doesn't need to build its own booking system at all; it can deep-link the patient straight into that specialist's existing HotDoc booking page with the referral pre-attached the moment the referral is created, removing the phone call without you having to build or maintain a booking engine.

**NHS e-RS's model (referenced in the earlier UK research) ties referral and booking into a single workflow at the point of referral**, rather than leaving booking as a separate step the patient has to chase up afterward — the GP or patient can select an appointment slot as part of completing the referral itself.

**A realistic build order, in three tiers:**
1. **Structured booking request, not a phone call.** Even before any deep integration, replacing "here's a letter, please call this number" with a structured request in the platform (patient details, urgency, availability) that goes to the specialist's practice — by secure message or even just a well-formatted email/fax equivalent — gives the specialist's front desk something to act on and gives the patient a trackable status ("request sent → acknowledged → booked → confirmed") instead of silence. This is buildable immediately and doesn't require any specialist practice's cooperation beyond receiving a message, which is the same secure-messaging pattern already planned for referrals themselves.
2. **Deep-link into existing booking platforms where the specialist already has one.** HotDoc and HealthEngine both already have online booking live in a meaningful share of specialist practices — rather than competing with them, detect which platform a given specialist uses and hand the patient straight into that flow with the referral attached, as described above.
3. **True API-to-API booking** — your platform requests a slot, the specialist's system confirms it automatically — is the end state, but it requires the same kind of vendor partnership work as the GP-practice-software integration discussed earlier (with HotDoc/HealthEngine specifically, or with individual specialist practice management systems), and is worth pursuing once you have enough referral volume to be a partner worth building for rather than a startup asking for a favour.

## How to actually source the directory data: sync-and-cache, not scan-and-scrape — and each source needs different treatment

Short answer: no scanning or scraping of any of these, but for three different reasons depending on the source — and the right architecture is a locally cached register your app actually queries, kept in sync on a schedule, not a live call out to someone else's site on every search.

**NHSD — build the sync architecture around its official API.** This is the one source actually meant to be consumed this way: pull data through the Consumer API on a scheduled batch job (nightly is a reasonable default, since practice locations and consulting patterns don't change hour to hour) into your own database, with each record tagged with its source and last-synced timestamp. Your app then queries your own fast local copy, not Healthdirect's API on every patient search — that also means a Healthdirect outage doesn't take your specialist search down with it. One thing I couldn't confirm from public documentation: the published NHSD developer guide PDF link is dead, so the exact caching permissions, any required refresh interval, rate limits, and attribution requirements aren't confirmed — that's a genuine open question, added to the running regulator-questions list for a direct answer from Healthdirect's developer team rather than assumed.

**AHPRA — not a bulk-sync target at all; use it for individual, point-in-time verification instead.** AHPRA offers two very different things, and neither is "scan the whole register and refresh daily": a free public search for looking up one practitioner at a time (the right tool for verifying a specialist's registration when they join the platform — a spot-check logged to that specialist's record, not something to run in bulk against), and a formal, paid **National Register extract or copy service** for organisations that genuinely need bulk data — $2,000 per board per point-in-time snapshot, a mandatory Data Use Agreement, delivery by secure SFTP within 5 business days, and explicit restriction that the data "cannot be shared with another organisation or used for any other purpose than that for which it is requested." ([AHPRA — National Register extract or copy](https://www.ahpra.gov.au/Registration/Employer-Services/External-data-exchange-services/National-Register-Copy-or-Extract.aspx)) That's a one-off licensed snapshot, not a continuously refreshed feed — repurchasing it daily or even monthly to keep a live directory current would be prohibitively expensive and isn't what the service is designed for. Worth noting as a signal, too: there's a third-party "AHPRA Practitioner Scraper" listed on the Apify scraper marketplace marked **[DEPRECATED]** — evidence that unauthorised automated scraping of AHPRA has already been tried and didn't hold up, which is one more reason not to go that route.

**Commercial directories (HealthShare, HotDoc, HealthEngine) — don't scan these at all.** These are potential competitors, almost certainly with terms of service that explicitly prohibit automated scraping, and unauthorised access carries real legal exposure (breach of contract at minimum, potentially computer-misuse offences depending on how access controls are framed). The only legitimate path to their data is a formal licensing or partnership agreement — which circles back to the earlier recommendation to treat these as a later data-quality upgrade to pursue from a position of negotiating strength, not a background data source to quietly build on.

**One more architectural point worth building in from the start: self-maintained listings supersede synced ones.** NHSD/AHPRA data will always have gaps and staleness for any specific specialist — the moment a specialist actually activates on the platform (connects a calendar, starts receiving referrals through the booking module), let them correct and own their own listing directly, and treat that as authoritative over whatever came from the synced government source. That's more accurate, better UX, and it's also the natural on-ramp from "we found this specialist in a directory" to "this specialist is now an active user" — which is the actual growth loop for the platform, not just a data-quality detail.

**And to be clear about what this sync architecture is *not* for:** actual open appointment slots are a completely different, much-faster-changing data type, handled by the booking module's calendar sync described below, not by this directory refresh cycle. Don't conflate "where does this specialist practice and roughly when" (fine to refresh daily) with "what's actually free on their calendar right now" (needs to be live or near-live, via the calendar-sharing mechanism, not a daily batch job).

## Decision: build a native booking module (not just deep-link out)

Agreed this should be a core module rather than a thin layer pointing elsewhere — a real two-sided scheduling system is exactly the kind of thing that makes a patient, carer, GP, *and* specialist all open the platform repeatedly rather than just at referral time, which is the stickiness case for building rather than just linking out to HotDoc/HealthEngine where they already exist. The deep-link approach from above is still worth keeping as a fallback for specialist practices that won't share a calendar or won't be integrated any time soon — but the native module is the right long-term core.

## How calendar sharing should actually work, and a real constraint to design around

Checked what the incumbent practice software actually exposes today, because "the specialist shares their calendar" needs to mean something concrete. The finding is worth knowing before committing to an architecture: **Zedmed does not currently expose real-time appointment availability through any third-party API or webhook** — its online booking ("OLA") is a closed loop, patients book only through Zedmed's own portal, reception then reconciles it back into Zedmed's own records. ([Zedmed — Set up Online Appointments](https://help.zedmed.com.au/help/set-up-online-appointments)) There's no strong reason to expect Best Practice, MedicalDirector, or Genie to be meaningfully more open on this specific point without direct confirmation — appointment books are exactly the kind of core, monetised feature a practice-software vendor has the least incentive to open up to a third party. So "if the specialist shares their calendar" needs a path that doesn't depend on winning a data-sharing negotiation with each PMS vendor before you can ship anything.

**The practical near-term path: standard calendar-sharing protocols, not PMS integration.** Ask the specialist or GP to connect (or simply paste in a feed URL from) whatever calendar they already use day-to-day — Google Calendar, Outlook/Microsoft 365, or any standard CalDAV/ICS feed — for free/busy visibility. This is exactly the mechanism scheduling tools like Calendly use to get real availability without needing the underlying practice-management system's cooperation at all, and it means a specialist can start using the booking module the same day, independent of which PMS their practice runs. Treat deeper native integration with each PMS's appointment book (where and if a vendor ever opens one up) as a longer-term upgrade layered on top of this, not a blocker to shipping v1.

**Design this as two-way, not read-only, from the start.** Reading free/busy tells you which slots to offer, but the moment a patient books, that booking has to land back in the specialist's real system of record too — otherwise you've solved "don't offer an already-taken slot" but created a new problem where the practice's own calendar doesn't know about the booking your platform just made. In practice that means: write the confirmed booking back as a calendar event on whatever calendar was connected (all three of Google, Outlook, and CalDAV support this cleanly), and additionally push a confirmation through the same secure-messaging channel already used for referrals so reception has a positive, trackable notification rather than relying on the calendar sync alone. Where a genuine PMS-native write-API exists in future (worth another direct question in the same vendor conversations already planned), that becomes the authoritative version instead of the calendar-sync workaround.

## Preference-based matching — a real, proven pattern worth borrowing directly

Checked how Zocdoc — the closest large-scale analogue, a two-sided patient/provider booking marketplace with its own developer API — actually does this, since it's the best available reference architecture rather than guessing at one from scratch.

**Zocdoc's provider side is exactly the shape this module needs**: providers "create and manage provider availability schedules" through the API, and the platform "receives real-time updates when appointments are booked," while the consumer side lets patients "search within [the] provider network based on insurance, specialty, location, and availability" and pulls back "real timeslots." ([Zocdoc for Developers](https://api-docs.zocdoc.com/guides)) That's the two-sided API shape to design toward: specialist/GP side manages an availability schedule and gets booking events pushed to it; patient side searches and filters against real slots, not a static directory.

**On the matching logic itself, worth being precise about what Zocdoc actually optimises for versus what you're proposing.** Zocdoc's own published ranking leans heavily on soonest availability, insurance match, and proximity — it lets patients filter by day/time availability but the day-of-week/time-of-day *preference-driven matching* idea is more specific than what Zocdoc's search does today. ([How Search Works — Zocdoc](https://www.zocdoc.com/about/how-search-works/)) That's fine — it just means this is a genuine point of differentiation rather than a copy of an existing feature, not a gap in the idea. Concretely:

- Patient sets a preference profile at referral time (or once, reusable across future referrals): preferred days of week, and a broad time-of-day band (morning/afternoon/evening, or a specific window if they want to be precise) — keep the input simple, a grid or a couple of dropdowns, not a complex form.
- When the specialist's (or GP's) connected calendar has open slots, the matching engine ranks them against the patient's preference profile and surfaces the best-fit options first, rather than a flat chronological list.
- The specialist or their reception staff can equally propose specific slots against the same preference data — "the patient wants Tuesday/Thursday afternoons, here are three that fit" — turning what's currently a back-and-forth phone negotiation into a short list either side can just pick from.
- **Automatic waitlist matching is the natural extension, and it's worth building in from the start rather than bolting on later**: if a preferred slot opens up later — a cancellation, a schedule change — the patient (or a specialist's waitlisted patient generally) gets proactively notified and can claim it in one tap. This is a well-established pattern for exactly this reason: Zocdoc runs a dedicated waitlist-management feature specifically to fill cancellations faster, because an unfilled slot is lost revenue for the provider and lost access for whichever patient could have taken it. ([Patient Waitlist Management — Zocdoc](https://www.zocdoc.com/resources/blog/article/how-to-improve-patient-waitlist-management-and-fill-cancellations-faster/)) That gives you a second, independent pitch to specialist practices beyond the referral-routing story: this module actively reduces their unfilled/cancelled slots, which is a direct revenue argument for them, not just a convenience feature for the patient.
